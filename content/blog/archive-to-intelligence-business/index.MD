A national news outlet kept over a decade of the country's political record in one place, and couldn't ask it a single question. I built the machinery that turns that dead archive into structured, queryable records, then into an intelligence product a commercial team can sell. "Everything this person said about this policy since launch" now comes back in under a second, with a citation on every row.

## Why this matters to you

Years of published work add up to a dataset, and a dataset has buyers who'll never read your articles: lobbyists, compliance teams, embassies, investors. Someone whose job depends on your information will pay many times what a reader pays, and they pay it out of a company budget. Right now your archive earns once, at publication, and never again.

My client sat on 500,000+ articles, most tags stuck on a single article and tens of thousands carrying none, while "related articles" returned noise. The front of the site draws tens of millions of pageviews a month; behind it sits a record nobody can question.

## What it costs to sit on it

Every year the archive gets bigger, and every year it earns the same: nothing. You pay to store a proprietary record of a country's politics, while the buyers who would pay for structured access to it get that intelligence somewhere else. They sit on a budget line you already reach through advertising, and the margin on an intelligence product beats any ad campaign. That revenue stays on the table for as long as the archive stays a pile of prose.

## What actually helps

Structure first: every article becomes rows (who said what, which vote, which seat, which stance) resolved across the whole corpus, so "everything this person said about this policy since launch" becomes a query that returns in under a second, instead of half a day of digging. Then monitoring, so new articles land already structured and the dataset stays current with no data-entry headcount. Then drafting: a weekly political tracker, regulatory monitoring, sector briefings, election intelligence, each draft cited back to its source and signed off by your own journalists so it carries their name.

The model is documented. Politico Pro took a beat the newsroom already covered and sold it to people whose job depends on it: roughly half of Politico's revenue, around $15,000 per contract, about 90% renewal. The Economist Intelligence Unit runs the same play.
